While taking a shower just now, I thought of a question that had occurred to me a long time ago.

I was thinking (in a more general sense) about the time and space in which we live. We have gradually built a theoretical system based on mathematics (mathematics provides the methodology for reasoning + experiments and measurements provide the relevant constants). Through such a system, we can predict many phenomena.

There are two situations here:

1. When measurement of the initial state, modeling techniques, and computing power are all up to standard, we find that we can predict results almost perfectly accurately. (Simply put, given speed and distance, we can predict arrival time.)
2. But we also find some things we cannot measure, such as whether the atom of a radioactive element has decayed after a specific period of time. (Of course, there is still academic debate over whether this is truly unmeasurable or merely impossible to measure accurately.)

So I started wondering: doesn't this suggest that we all live under [fatalism](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%AE%BF%E5%91%BD%E8%AE%BA)? The more I thought about it, the more it seemed like that might really be the case: in most situations, we just don't have sufficiently precise initial-state measurement technology, modeling techniques, or computing power.

What example should I give? Suppose I could measure the states of all atoms on Earth (or quarks, or whatever other fundamental particles there are), and suppose I had a magnificent model and an incomparably powerful computer. Then I could probably predict how Earth would develop in the absence of extraterrestrial interference: from things as large as human economic crises to things as small as what password Alice and Bob would use next to communicate.

A bit terrifying when you think about it.

Because it felt a bit terrifying, I started thinking about other possibilities (speaking as a human being who likes breadth and completeness).

BTW, I actually agree with the theory that some things cannot be measured.

So then I thought: if I added that kind of ability, the final result of running it might just be measuring a random constant of the universe (earlier I talked about Earth; here I'm switching by analogy to the universe).

Speaking of random constants, what I actually want to talk about is how random numbers are implemented in computers: basically, by looking up a table.

If you think about it this way, then perhaps not everything is predestined after all. Everything returns to a question of probability.

Uh, actually, you could also say it is predestined. It's just that that destiny is the random number table—only, aside from the creator (no religion or god implied here), no one knows what that random number table looks like. In other words, no one knows whether it follows a normal distribution—or whether it is fair. But anyway, it seems to be what causes most things around us to follow normal distributions.

(Bathroom thoughts are messy and quick, so I won't explain in detail the terms involved.)

That's all.
