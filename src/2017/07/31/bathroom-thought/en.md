title: "Bathroom Thoughts"
date: 2017-07-31 21:17:00 +0800
---

While showering just now, I remembered a question I thought about long ago.

I was thinking (in a more general way): in this spacetime we inhabit, we gradually build theory systems grounded in mathematics (mathematics provides inference methods + experiments provide constants). With such systems, we can predict many phenomena.

There seem to be two cases:

1. When measurement of initial states, modeling techniques, and computing power are all sufficient, we can predict outcomes almost perfectly. (Simple example: from speed and distance we can predict arrival time.)
2. But we also encounter things we cannot measure well, such as whether a radioactive atom has decayed after a specific time. (Whether this is fundamentally unmeasurable or just currently immeasurable is still debated.)

So I wondered: does this imply we live in [determinism](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%AE%BF%E5%91%BD%E8%AE%BA)? The more I thought about it, the more plausible it felt. In most cases, we just lack sufficiently precise initial-state measurement, modeling, and computation.

For example, if I could measure the state of every atom on Earth (or quark, or whatever fundamental particle), and had a great model plus an unbelievably powerful computer, I might predict Earth's development (without extraterrestrial interference): from large-scale events like economic crises to tiny details like what cipher Alice and Bob will use.

That sounds scary.

Because that felt scary, I started thinking about other possibilities (as a supposedly broad-minded human).

BTW, I personally lean toward the "cannot fully measure" side.

Then I thought: even with that predictive capability, the final result might still reduce to measuring random constants of a universe (earlier I used Earth; now I am extending the analogy).

Speaking of random constants, I am really thinking of random-number implementation in computers: essentially lookup tables.

With that perspective, maybe not everything is predetermined. The problem returns to probability.

Or maybe it is still predetermined—the "fate" is just that random table. Except for the creator (no religious implication here), nobody knows what that table looks like—whether it is normal-distributed, fair, or not. But anyway, it seems to produce many approximately normal-distributed outcomes for us.

(These are bathroom thoughts—messy and fast—so I will not explain all terms in detail.)

That's all.
