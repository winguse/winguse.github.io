## Vision

Some time ago I read [https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/24214731](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/24214731), and human vision suddenly felt much clearer to me. Similarly, I had previously also read [https://www.byvoid.com/zht/blog/high-dimensional-world-and-unified-value](https://www.byvoid.com/zht/blog/high-dimensional-world-and-unified-value). In fact, cognition is nothing more than projecting an originally high-dimensional vector onto some low-dimensional plane.

What we call human vision is, in reality, a three-dimensional cognition of one particular band within the electromagnetic waves reflected by objects. I used to be very puzzled: why is it that, though both are electromagnetic waves, mobile phone signals can pass through a wooden board while the light we see cannot? Now, if I think about it the other way around, for the frequency band of mobile phone signals, a wooden board is really just like the lenses we use in everyday life.

I also looked up some material on color blindness. In fact, color-blind people merely lack one type of visual cell or differ from us in some way, which causes them to see things differently. Looking further, I discovered the existence of tetrachromacy: in the eyes of people with tetrachromatic vision, the things we paint may look like complete chaos, because within what we consider a single color, they can distinguish many more variations.

## Artificial Intelligence

In 2016, many people defined it as the first year of artificial intelligence. In a New Year's speech in 2017, Luoji Siwei devoted a large section to it as well. However, I somewhat feel that this is a kind of bubble phenomenon. Many of Luo Pang's arguments are conclusions or inferences drawn from a humanities student's rather shallow understanding of science and engineering.

Sometimes I feel that artificial intelligence can replace part of human skill, but not all of it, and probably not all of it. Simply put, repetitive and mechanical work will absolutely be replaced—they are all low-dimensional tasks. But creative activities are much harder to replace; their dimensionality is too high for current computer technology to handle.

AlphaGo burst onto the scene and crushed players from every corner. Still, I only classify this kind of artificial intelligence as a low-dimensional problem that is within reach. Go, at the end of the day, is only a problem of scale `2^361`. Although that number is indeed very large, compared with some of our creative work its dimensionality is quite small. For example, in any software engineering project, all the `if` and `else` branches certainly cannot be described by so simple a thing as 361 dimensions. What's more, in terms of the essence of solving the problem of winning and losing in Go, both humans and computers are still only approximately solving it. Both sides are reducing Go into a lower-dimensional problem (in human terms, estimating how many moves the other side might make); it's just that the dimension computers can handle after reduction is much larger than what humans can accept. So when computers defeat humans in the single field of Go, we shouldn't jump to sweeping conclusions.

I'm not trying to belittle artificial intelligence here. Sometimes machines can give humans a lot of inspiration in this respect, and can even fundamentally change human cognition. We have to admit that the understanding accumulated by our ancestors over generations has its limitations. Human evolution is a highly random process; we were simply selected and thus became what we are today, and we have little ability to control the evolution of our own species. What we can control is only that we mate, as much as possible, with outstanding members of the opposite sex (laughs) (just kidding). By contrast, machine evolution, under human intervention and control, advances along a route that is more stable and more inclined toward correctness. For example, with Go, machine solving may overturn our understanding of many game records because machines perform deeper calculations (their computational dimension is higher than that of humans).

There are indeed some things artificial intelligence will do better than humans. I think, for example, diagnosis and treatment: if much of human medical knowledge is given to artificial intelligence, I believe that in the future computers will make better diagnostic judgments than many doctors. Why? Because current diagnosis and treatment is, in a certain sense, repetitive work—an experience-based job. Machines are far superior to humans in measurement accuracy, statistical ability over historical data, and memory. Of course, machines can have bugs too, so before they are perfected, human doctors remain indispensable.

Another example is writing. I think that if it is merely a matter of writing some dramatic content, machines will replace humans very quickly. In my eyes, piecing together similar plots and soap-opera-like storylines is, in reality, work of very low dimensionality. For work like that, machines will obviously do better than people.

Working in the computer software industry, I've also heard many discussions about the day machines replace humans in writing code. Yet I feel even more strongly that this understanding is rather shallow. Suppose that one day machines really can replace humans in writing code: humans describe a requirement, and the machine can produce the corresponding program. At that point, what it would more likely mean is that humans have mastered another way of telling machines what they should do at a given time. Perhaps then we could only say that humans had become able to write code more easily, not that humans no longer needed to write code. The ideal case would be that you speak in natural language and the machine understands, but I don't think the dimensionality of that problem is something current machines can handle. And the final product would not be something humans could accept either.

Taking the discussion further, I feel that under the current background of computer science, artificial intelligence has a ceiling. The ultimate result of this current wave of AI enthusiasm will depend on the computational limits of silicon chips; put another way, how much a company ultimately achieves depends on how much computational power it possesses. Setting aside the development of AI technology itself (assuming AI algorithms have already become extremely sophisticated), the computer architecture of the past century or so (the von Neumann computer) has determined that the dimensionality of the problems it can solve is limited. Everyone is discussing neural networks, deep neural networks, convolutional neural networks, yet using binary silicon-based systems to simulate real carbon-based biological neurons is destined to stop at some finite dimensionality. I don't have an exact number for how many dimensions short it falls, because I'm only expressing a feeling—and my feeling is that the gap is enormous, by several orders of magnitude. And the key point is that as the dimensionality of a problem increases, complexity grows exponentially rather than linearly. I think anyone formally trained in computer science feels this deeply. I would say this is an NP-complete problem: you can approximate it, but once the dimensionality becomes large enough, approximate solutions are of little use. Perhaps one day computers will first crack the RSA algorithm, the cornerstone of our information security, before it comes time to replace humans in writing code (a programmer's mysterious flex).

Of course, I would still say that even if development continues like this, current artificial intelligence will still bring many good things to humanity. After all, the results of this kind of simulation are somewhat higher in dimensionality than humans' own understanding of a given problem. Current silicon-based computers still have plenty of room left for humans to squeeze more out of them.

It's just that if we expect artificial intelligence to truly surpass humanity, I still think that is possible. But it is absolutely not something humanity can achieve at its current level of technology. Perhaps our computer field will need an innovation that is fundamentally different in nature. Perhaps quantum computers, biological computers; perhaps neither of those, and perhaps we still need to wait for some physical breakthrough (I actually think chemistry and biology are both only subfields of physics) to trigger the next technological revolution. This is destined not to be something in the near future. As for me, I also hope I can live that long.

Given the current momentum, then, what judgment should an individual make? I think everyone should soberly realize that only work with creativity will not be eliminated by society, and everyone must keep learning and improving at all times. I think most people already know this, so I won't expand on it. This principle did not emerge because of artificial intelligence; every stage of society is similar, and AI has only made this truth feel more profound. But I also feel that not everyone has a correct understanding of which jobs are truly creative. For example, accounting, cashier work, and various counter services are very easily fully replaced; architectural design, for example, may also be largely replaced... Of course, I'm just thinking casually. After all, I do not have a complete understanding of any particular industry, and I may be wrongly judging their dimensionality to be low. And some things are not something artificial intelligence alone can handle; you may need support from many other disciplines, such as mechanics and power systems. Haven't machines been plagued by battery life for the past decade or so?

## Some Other Thoughts

On Zhihu, I followed this question: [https://www.zhihu.com/question/31637520](https://www.zhihu.com/question/31637520). Sometimes I feel that when a person's understanding becomes deeper, it becomes too easy to see through certain things, and that isn't necessarily a good thing. Put simply, perhaps that's called being picky; or perhaps it means you understand more and therefore know better how to choose, and what is not suitable for you. The differences in people's cognition are just too great. To know a person, you need to understand too many aspects of them, and that is also a very tiring process.

Sometimes I also feel that this is a rise of rationality and a loss of sensibility: a rise of rationality can make one lose the original heart of sensibility.
