---
title: "Information Should Flow Freely"
date: 2017-03-27 09:35:00 +0800
---

As for information, my view is that I support its free flow. So I sometimes especially loathe things that obstruct the flow of information, such as:

1. WeChat official accounts never open RSS and employ all kinds of anti-scraping measures. In fact, Tencent products all have this petty style, obsessively building their own closed loops and keeping all the benefits for themselves: you want to scrape data? No way. Even if users want to export their own data, forget it (anything that can only be exported in an encrypted format and only imported back to the same place doesn't count). It's disgusting to the extreme.
2. Zhihu Live becomes more expensive after it ends. This obstructs the continued spread of knowledge that has already been created. In essence, the later distribution no longer carries production costs, and some knowledge even depreciates over time. The same goes for a whole bunch of Q&A products. Here, I want to give Weibo Q&A some credit: it costs only 1 yuan to look on.

I know some people will argue against my view, saying that none of the above have any obligation to provide such services and that they are, in essence, commercial products. I agree with that. The world is vast, and everyone has things they like or dislike. As for me, I simply dislike them. But I'm not the kind of person who gets violated and then chooses to enjoy it; I have my own values and principles. Put more broadly, I think they have failed to fulfill the public responsibility they should bear. After all, much of that information is publicly published, yet their products deliberately restrict the circulation of that public information.

I don't think these things necessarily need to be provided for free. Even the GPL license contains wording about redistribution fees:

> URL: https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-2.0.txt
> In section 3.b:
>
> … for a charge no more than your cost of physically performing source distribution …

As commercial companies, there is nothing wrong with charging for services. What I'm criticizing here is the behavior of refusing to do it, and deliberately imposing restrictions. Sometimes you can tell a company's attitude toward information flow just by looking at its robots.txt. I somewhat feel that many domestic companies lack this kind of openness.

Finally, some people may say, don't other companies do the same? In response, first, I don't enjoy being violated; second, perhaps you simply haven't seen enough. You can look at how some benchmark companies do things: for example, Medium also outputs RSS; Google and Facebook both provide RSS and allow users to export data.

Anyway, you don't have to agree with my view, and I never asked you to. It's just that this is my value system.

So stated.
