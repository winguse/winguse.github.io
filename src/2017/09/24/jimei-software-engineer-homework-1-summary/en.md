I finally finished reading all 60 overdue blog posts today, so here are some brief notes.

As the saying goes, “Never bully a poor young man.” In my comments, I tried not to sound too absolute, but some remarks may still have been discouraging. Sorry — that is admittedly one of my bad habits. But I really do not look down on anyone because of that. And to the students who felt discouraged, please do not take it too much to heart. I also look forward to you proving me wrong with your achievements.

## How everyone got involved with computers

Most of the cases I saw were more or less accidental. Fewer than one in ten students truly entered this field because of their own interests or hobbies. If we look at this more broadly, it probably also has to do with how our basic education suppresses students’ individuality too much, leaving them unclear about what they actually like.

Among those who do have related interests, many came because of video games. To be honest, that is not surprising. But for them, the gap may also be quite large after they get in, because learning a skill and playing games are really very different things.

Another type of student came because they or their families saw factors such as “easy to find a job” or “high salary” in the computer industry. In fact, this is true in many industries — because **as long as you are outstanding, jobs will always look for you rather than the other way around**. Although I have not done any formal statistics, from what I have observed (in universities and in campus recruitment interviews I participated in), at least half of computer science graduates — even those from key undergraduate schools — have difficulty finding jobs, and many ultimately do not work in related fields. Computing is, after all, a sunrise industry with huge demand for talent. But if you reflect on why there are so many graduates while the market still has a big gap, the answer is simple: what is lacking is always talent for high-end positions; low-end talent is always in oversupply.

The last type of student is those who, because of reassignment or various accidents, say very clearly: “I don’t like computers,” “I’m not good at it,” or “I won’t work in this field in the future.” This is a rather awkward situation. Universities do provide some possibility of changing majors, but it seems they may not have that chance anymore. I do not especially want to persuade them to keep struggling in a field they dislike, but I do feel that if they just drift through college for a bachelor’s degree, it is a bit of a waste of youth. At the same time, I sensed a lot of negative emotions in their writing: fear of difficulty, giving up, seeking the comfort of just getting by... In your early twenties, there are still many variables in the future. What worries me is that, for many people with this mindset, this is just an excuse — I rather suspect that even if this were a field they were interested in, they would still sink into decadence. There is no easy road in this world. We are all adults; we have to be responsible for ourselves.

Gold will shine wherever it is.

## Understanding the computer industry

I have worked for more than four years now. Reading so much writing also reminded me of my own earlier days. Back then, I also did not know that real life and work in computing would look like this.

From several female students’ blogs, I read their reasons (or prejudices) for deciding not to work in the computer industry: they thought most people in computing are bald, work overtime, and are somehow shady...

In fact, baldness is mostly because this industry has more men. When men reach middle age, baldness is always a possibility. Besides, smart men tend to have strong testosterone, right? As for overtime, that really depends on the person and the company. In my past few years of work, I have not had much overtime at all. On the contrary, I especially like the flexible schedules and arrangements in tech companies. As for being shady, I did not see that at either Microsoft or Tubi.TV. At Microsoft, there were also plenty of attractive female colleagues — what do you think?

As for why outsiders have such prejudice, perhaps it is simply because programmers have become highly visible in recent years and active in the public eye, so they became targets for jokes. Hmm, although many of those jokes are indeed pretty good, that is not the whole picture. From another angle, programmers get mocked precisely because computing is deeply affecting everyone’s life.

I would actually like to tell students more about what this industry is really like, but that may be a bit redundant, since *The Method of Constructing Software* already covers it. In the end, you still have to read carefully, investigate carefully, and practice seriously by yourselves. You are already juniors now; next year it will be time to prepare a good résumé and transcript, knock on the door of software company internships, and see it for yourselves.

## Complaints about college

What students complained about most was having more than a hundred people crammed into one classroom without air conditioning... I am only an online teaching assistant whom you have never met, so there is nothing I can do about that. Perhaps Teacher Su can help.

As for the rest — complaints about how bad the school is in various ways — I will not go into detail. I did not come from a famous university either, so I understand that when a school gives you limited resources for growth, you naturally have complaints. But I still think it depends more on yourself. One senior from ACM/ICPC once said something like this: when we go out to compete, we definitely cannot beat the very best teams from Tsinghua or Peking University; but if we can beat even one of their teams, is that not already impressive? At least, we are not worse than all of them. — And then, we did it. Even after I started working, I still feel the same way: I can confidently say that I am not worse than all of them; in fact, I might even say I am better than most of them — and if I am not there yet, then I will just keep working harder.

When we graduated from high school, our class monitor once said something I found quite interesting: let us be ascetics walking in the ivory tower. University does not mean liberation. To be frank, I always felt I did not have enough time in college, and looking back now, there were many things I could have done better. In life, the most depressing thing is not that someone is smarter than you, but that someone smarter than you is also more hardworking than you.

Alright, enough chicken soup.

## About MOOCs and mathematics

When answering about the difference between coming from MOOCs and coming from a formal program, and about why there are so many math courses, everyone’s views were actually fairly similar. People generally felt that university can give more foundational education, and that mathematics can train thinking ability.

To be honest, even among practicing engineers there are discussions like this. In my daily work, I write some new features, implement some business logic, and do some tests — what the heck does that have to do with data structures, algorithms, or complexity? And quite a number of working engineers are not all that familiar with those things either. If I had to describe my feeling, the situations at work where the algorithms and such learned in a full undergraduate education are really used may genuinely be less than 10%. But does that really mean they are useless? Actually, this is a very clear dividing line between two kinds of people. There are two aspects to it: on one hand, for some key business problems, engineers who understand not just the how but also the why can make the most correct choices; on the other hand, engineers with solid fundamentals usually also have stronger overall qualities (perseverance, learning ability, intelligence).

As for mathematics, from my own experience, I really regret not having learned it better. For example, to guarantee service availability, based on the probability of a single machine being available, you can calculate how many machines are needed to guarantee 99.99%; with discrete mathematics, you can learn and derive cryptographic principles; to parse expressions entered by users, you need compiler theory, and so on (these are only examples I have actually encountered at work). I feel that everything in this world can be explained and derived through mathematics — including programming languages. Mathematicians have even defined [languages that can prove their own correctness](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coq).

## About the future

As I said above, students still have too limited an understanding of the real situation in computing. As a result, in many cases, their plans for the future seem very immature.

By the way, most students have not even realized what the professionalism of computing really means. I think a very important idea is to shift from simply using computer software (or playing games) to creating new software. Back in the first year at Jimei University, there seemed to be lots of teaching around software like PhotoShop and Flash. Why does everyone’s expectation for their career somehow turn into doing that kind of work?

In discussions of the future, the keyword “civil servant” appeared many times. Usually, I would say that the national civil service exam becoming an important career path for university graduates is really a tragedy for Chinese universities. It seems everyone is thinking: government work, good pay, stable, comfortable... But then who will do real industry? Frankly speaking, this is a social issue. If everyone enters government departments for stability and comfort, what does that mean? Government becomes inefficient, more civil servants are needed, more taxes are collected, industry has an even harder time, and more people go become civil servants... To put it bluntly, becoming a civil servant for stability and comfort really makes it feel like all that studying was for nothing. Students who have paid attention to social phenomena might compare the proportion of officials in China with that in other countries.

And finally, are you worried that your future job, because of its repetitive and low-skill nature, will be replaced by machines?
