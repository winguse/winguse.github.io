## Airplanes

Actually, plane tickets to the U.S. are not expensive at all these days. During the National Day holiday, I went traveling in Xinjiang, and the round-trip airfare cost more than 4,000 RMB. But this time, the round-trip from Beijing to San Francisco was still under 4,000, which was honestly a bit jaw-dropping. The time on the plane is about 11 to 12 hours. Flying from Beijing there is a bit shorter than flying back. I think this is because of the wind belts in the stratosphere: around 40 degrees north latitude there are westerlies, which cover most of the route, while near the poles there are polar easterlies. United Airlines shows related data on board, which confirmed this. It felt like if the plane did not first need to fly north and pass through the polar easterlies, the difference would be even greater. Winds in the stratosphere are around 100 km/h, so the speed difference between a tailwind and a headwind can reach 200 km/h.

![The plane's contrail and the morning sun before landing](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5042.jpg)

You can leave your phone on during the flight! And you can use it to connect to Wi-Fi and watch movies from the in-flight server, or pay to access the internet via satellite.

## Traffic order

Traffic rules in the U.S. are pretty similar to those in China: cars drive on the right, and many road signs are basically the same. The two things that felt especially different to me were Stop Signs and the fact that bicycles have the same right of way as cars. Hmm, it also seems like the U.S. does not really have a separate category called traffic police—they are all just police.

![Stop sign, cars must yield to pedestrians](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5197.jpg)

![Inside San Francisco](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5271.jpg)

Public transportation in the U.S. is not very developed; San Francisco is already considered relatively good. But compared with the brand-new subway systems in China, their metro can only be called shabby. They call it BART.

![Subway carriage](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5120.jpg)

![Subway train](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5122.jpg)

The Bay Area is huge—at least much bigger than I had imagined. One weekend I went to the South Bay to see a former colleague and took the train; it was a full 80 kilometers. One thing worth mentioning is that Caltrain's ticketing is really relaxed. You buy the ticket yourself at the station entrance and then board the train on your own. At the station where I got off there was no staff at all. I heard there are inspectors on the train, but I never ran into one. It relies entirely on honesty. Of course, if you are caught, the fine is extremely high. A small anecdote: when I went in the morning, I accidentally bought two tickets. The station staff signed one of them and said I could use it to come back in the evening, and then I really did use it to come back. I really like this kind of environment built on trust.

![A station where no one checks tickets after getting off](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5211.jpg)

## National parks

This time I went to Yosemite National Park, about 300 km east of the Bay Area, and it takes about 3 hours to drive there. There is an entrance fee for the national park, something like 20 dollars per car. Compared with scenic spot tickets in China, honestly, that is dirt cheap (despite higher income levels, the price is lower).

![The low ticket price of the national park](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5338.jpg)

Yosemite provides free shuttle buses, and some routes are reserved for shuttle buses only. We went on a weekend and saw quite a few people out camping. There is a supermarket inside the park, and prices are actually no different from outside the park. So honestly, compared with scenic spots in China, it really changed my perception a bit. There are not many trash cans inside the park, but Americans are still quite conscientious, and the park is very clean. Worth mentioning are the trash cans there, which have special locks to stop animals (such as bears and deer) from rummaging through them.

![A trash can designed to stop animals from rummaging through it](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5383.jpg)

Yes, there really are deer and bears inside the park. Once we saw three deer right next to a shuttle stop. As for squirrels, they are even more common. The harmony between people and nature feels genuinely comfortable.

![A scene in Yosemite National Park](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5402.jpg)

Of course, there are things to complain about too. Not long after leaving the Bay Area and entering the mountains, there was basically no signal at all. Even in Yosemite, only T-Mobile sometimes had service. That was really annoying. We had gone in two cars, and because communication was difficult, we ended up splitting up and acting separately.

## So many Chinese people

San Francisco has had large numbers of Chinese residents since the late Qing dynasty, so it is not surprising that there are many Chinese people there. One Saturday, I wandered all over San Francisco and went to Chinatown. Chinese characters and Cantonese were everywhere. There is still a lot of variety among Chinese people. I did not observe much, but just to say a little about what I saw: in Chinatown, many older people seemed to speak only Cantonese; in some malls and in Chinese restaurants, I guessed some of the servers were ABCs and did not seem able to speak Chinese; and then there were many people like my colleagues, born in China and working in IT in the Bay Area. In the U.S., questions of legal status are something many of my colleagues care deeply about.

## Shopping spots for Chinese people

One weekend, I went with some colleagues who were also on the business trip to an outlet mall. I had no desire to shop, and before going I did not even know that the common Chinese name for outlet malls was simply a transliteration of "outlet." After arriving and seeing Chinese credit-card advertisements all over the place, I was amazed. It felt like being back in China.

![Chinese advertising sign at the outlet mall](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5123.jpg)

Speaking of shopping, the U.S. is actually quite impressive in terms of trust in and protection of consumers. If you feel something is unsuitable, you can exchange it with a very simple process, even if you have already used it. And both the buyer and the seller tend to think that this is only natural. Unfortunately, one ugly phenomenon is that some Chinese people who come here take advantage of exactly this kind of loophole.

## Homeless people

A colleague told me that one day, for some reason, the governor of California said that California welcomed homeless people, and so there are still a lot of homeless people on the streets. Homeless people are a destabilizing factor for public safety. In a place like the U.S., it is still better not to wander around at night. Carry 20 to 40 dollars in your pocket; if you get robbed, that is usually all they want, so do not be too reluctant to hand it over. It is not that the government provides them with no shelter—rather, they are unwilling to accept relief. As one colleague put it, in the U.S., if you are able-bodied, you will not live too badly. They just do not want to work, or they may also be taking drugs, which is why they usually only rob people for that small amount of money.

California is a blue state, and political correctness matters a lot.

## How bills are structured

When you buy something in the U.S., the listed price usually does not include tax, and then sales tax is added at checkout. I had an awkward moment because of this once: I was thirsty and went into a 7-Eleven to buy a bottle of water marked at $0.99. I took out a one-dollar bill and was about to leave (I figured I would just not bother with the one cent), and then the cashier awkwardly held onto the bottle. In fact, the final price of the water was $1.04. Well, in the end I had to pay by card. BTW, the U.S. really is a country where gasoline is cheaper than water. Hmm, gasoline is much cheaper than it is in China.

![An awkward moment at 7-Eleven](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5274.jpg)

In restaurants, some places include tax in the listed price and some do not. You also need to tip the server, usually 15 to 20%. Tips are the main source of income for servers, so when in Rome, do as the Romans do—this is something you should give. Besides, servers there really do serve you. In that respect, servers in China are nowhere close.

![A tip for dining](/images/2016-11-13-IMG_5234.jpg)

After settling the bill a few times in the U.S., I finally understood how credit cards are actually used there, and also began to understand why they were designed that way in the first place. After a meal, when you pay, the server gives you a small folder that contains an itemized list of what you ordered. You put your credit card inside, and the server takes it away. When they come back, the folder contains two receipts, one for the merchant and one for you. You write down the tip you want to give and the total, then sign it, and you also copy it onto your own receipt. Finally, the server takes away the merchant copy, and that ends the process. The first time they take away your credit card, it has already been charged, usually for the bill amount before tip. Before the day ends, they re-enter the bill, and when the charge is posted to your credit card (usually the next day), the actual amount then includes the tip.

## Small environmental details

When staying at hotels in the U.S., I ran into trouble on the first day: no slippers, no toothpaste, no toothbrush! Also, takeout boxes at restaurants are paper. The streets are clean, not like Beijing's streets with those small gray spit stains everywhere (some people think that is purely an air-quality issue, but I think it really is driven by civic behavior).

## Accessibility facilities

My simple observation in this area is that accessibility facilities in the U.S. are not just for show—they are truly used by real people. You genuinely see people with disabilities using them, and they really do make life easier, making it no longer so hard for them to go out. In contrast, in China you almost never see the people who need those facilities going out, and there are also many ridiculous tactile paving paths, ones casually occupied by others, and so on. A recent internet incident is also worth reflecting on: after Didi acquired Uber China, a new version of its app no longer supported VoiceOver on iOS, making it unusable for some people with disabilities. In the U.S. there is legislation in this area: commercial companies must provide accessible services, and the law is truly enforced. At the same time, the moral standards of society consciously respect and uphold such rules. In China, leaving aside the fact that there is no such legislation, there is not even that level of civic awareness.

## Startups in Silicon Valley

I have observed too little here, but there is one small thing I can write about: companies give employees stock options, and they genuinely work hard to honor those promises honestly. Talent flows very freely and on a large scale. Compared with companies in China, they are more willing to pay for services instead of building everything themselves, and likewise they are much more committed to legitimate software and reject pirated software (which really is the right path for a healthy cycle).

## Some other random thoughts

Body and soul—at least one of them should be on the road. After leaving Microsoft, honestly, it felt like stepping out of one environment and seeing something broader. What the next few years will look like no longer feels so fixed; everything is full of uncertainty. At Microsoft, I was still somewhat conservative and set in my ways.

Technically, a few days ago I read an article from the subscription account Fei's IT World, which mentioned that Google published papers on the "three carriages," and then the community built its own thing based on those ideas. It started out rough, but eventually became something pretty good. Yet although Google put out the ideas, in the end it did not gain the corresponding influence in the community. Today, you really cannot ignore the power of the community. What looks like an open-source movement tightening its belt is actually driving the trend of the whole industry. One reason I want to mention this is that after stepping outside Microsoft, you see that people are all trying to avoid Microsoft technologies. In startups, all I saw were Macs and Ubuntu machines across the board—well, actually, even Ubuntu was relatively rare. In the past year or two, Microsoft seems to have awakened somewhat. I am very optimistic about the open-sourcing of .NET Core. But Windows, without a good terminal, and with its ecosystem shrinking bit by bit, leaves me with a question: will the developers who left ever come back? Anyway, whether or not Microsoft was my former employer, I still like .NET very much, and I hope its community matures soon.

## A society friendlier to the middle class

I am still young, and I may not yet understand enough to discuss this topic with real precision. But for someone like me, a programmer, comparing China and the U.S., the U.S. is indeed a society friendlier to the middle class. In Beijing, the bad air, soaring housing prices, restrictions from the household registration system, opaque government behavior, high taxes without corresponding social welfare, and a dishonest society are all despair-inducing phenomena. So it is no surprise that so many people choose to leave, and those who leave often happen to be the capable ones. The so-called Chinese Dream, in the end, seems to be left only to those who play power games. Of course, the U.S. is not perfect either; every place has things that make people uncomfortable. Many Chinese people in the U.S. worry about their immigration status. And taxes in the U.S. are not low either. For example, in California, sales tax is as high as 8.75%, and there is also income tax. It is just that, compared with income levels, housing prices there are still lower than in Beijing. There is also healthcare—medical costs in China are still far lower than in the U.S. As someone who grew up in China, I lament all the disgusting, decayed things that never change there, and how much pressure young people are forced to bear. To quote Qin Chao, China is actually bureaucratic capitalism. Children distinguish right from wrong; adults only weigh interests—I am gradually coming to understand that too. Rather than claiming to serve the people, it would be better to be blunt about whose interests one is really backing. So I very much understand [this Zhihu answer](https://www.zhihu.com/question/49616652/answer/117098151).

Also, in China we are taught too much that individual interests are less important than collective interests. In reality, individual interests are not so humble; they too deserve respect and protection. I still strongly agree that everyone should contribute more to the collective good, but the spirit of contract is very important: you cannot sacrifice collective interests because of individual interests, and the reverse is not acceptable either.

## A society of trust

Trust makes this society simpler.

I originally wanted to merge this heading into the previous section, but I still wanted to split it out. The train tickets I mentioned earlier, the easy returns and exchanges when shopping, the actual practice of accessibility facilities, tipping, and the healthy cycle created by rejecting pirated software are all examples of social rules built on trust. But in China, these are hard to imagine. As has already happened, some Chinese aunties who went to the U.S. wore clothes for a while and then returned them, and the merchants still accepted the return—until over time the merchants also made their exchange rules more complicated. How many tactile paving paths are occupied in China? If tipping existed in China, people would probably just change the amount while you were not paying attention. How much domestic software was defeated by piracy rather than by imported software?

Bike sharing has become very popular recently. The first one I used was Mobike, with its all-aluminum frame and built-in GPS tracking. Unfortunately, how many people move those bikes for their own private use? How many deliberately vandalize them? Another one I used was ofo. Its lock was just an ordinary mechanical combination lock, and the password stayed the same each time. Some people simply spray-painted the bikes and took them for themselves, while others even started projects to collect the passwords...

In truth, so many conveniences have been destroyed by our own people.

I hate this kind of greed with a passion.

This could have been a very simple society, but ugly petty people have made it so complicated.

So-called rules are really something everyone should follow. I mentioned Stop Signs above. In the U.S., this is also written into traffic law, and violators can be fined several hundred dollars. But think about it the other way: how many places in the U.S. actually have surveillance cameras? Of course, many social rules in the U.S. are based on the assumption that you are a decent person, but if one day you are investigated and found to have broken them, the punishment you face is quite severe. That can be seen as part of the reason why the system works. Still, if no one followed the rules, there would never be enough police.

I hope that one day there will be a country like this, where people follow the rules and society is simple.

I was born into hard mode, and now I live in China. My simple hope is for sound legal constraints, strict enforcement of the law, and punishment without mercy.
