---
title: "Ineffective Social Connections"
date: 2016-05-16 14:47:38 +0000
---

A year ago, Yahoo Beijing R&D was shut down. 36Kr hosted an event, and I joined out of curiosity, adding some people I had never met before. I met one founder there, but his WeChat friend list was already full, so he could not add more people. Over the past year, I also met people looking for ways to run multiple WeChat instances just to increase friend capacity; Taobao even sells dedicated solutions, and Xiaomi now supports this too. This morning I read a public-account article, "Hello, Mr. Tree Hole," which made a similar point: adding many people does not mean you truly have the energy to communicate with them.

At any given time, people can only maintain a few hundred recognizable social ties. Modern tools help us build connections, but not all of them can stay active. Here are my rough layers of social relationships:

1. Strangers. Usually people do not build such ties, but it still happens—for example, adding a shop owner on WeChat during a promotion, or sending photos to a passerby. These ties are optional and low-stakes. In many cases, for self-protection, both sides avoid viewing Moments or sending messages.
2. Acquaintances by circumstance: met once, did something together, or are in the same industry. This is probably the largest category. Once the overlap ends, conversation often fades.
3. If both people are active on Moments (posting often, or at least reading), and share interests in articles, travel, or life, the relationship can become "like friends." Browsing social feeds as entertainment does not consume much energy.
4. If one side never posts or participates, the tie becomes close to the stranger model. People who avoid social feeds also tend to trigger fewer follow-up chats. If you do not know what the other person is doing, there is little basis for deeper interaction. Maybe one day they remember what you do and message you about something specific.
5. If someone is overly aggressive, or sends content the other side dislikes—especially annoying spam-like Moments—most people will mute them, or even delete them.
6. Ordinary classmates/friends. Slightly different from #2: unless necessary, you usually do not delete each other. In practice, not that different.
7. Best-friend level. You can send anything anytime, both sides like communicating, and messages do not require immediate replies—but they are remembered.

For me, outside best-friend relationships, conversations are usually initiated only with a reason. That is the simplest summary. But when chat history shows you talk much more, or the other side did not reply to your last message, deciding whether to continue becomes painful. You do not know whether they were busy, forgot, or simply dislike you now. At that point, the practical value of the relationship drops a lot.

One more note: some people are simply not sensitive to messages. They may accidentally ignore what you sent, then forget to reply. Honestly, that can be hard to deal with. For me, communication with them feels tiring. Of course, it is just personality—neither right nor wrong.

Relationships require effort from both sides. One person's preference alone cannot decide the direction. Even if one side strongly hopes for closeness, they can only try within limits and only while the other side does not mind.

That boundary is hard to define. In abstract terms, it means knowing when to stop. For someone as direct as me, the engineering analogy is a retry mechanism: each retry doubles the delay.

So yes: mutual understanding, let things happen naturally. Relationships cannot be forced. Rather than building many cold, useless connections, it is better to invest in real friendships.

Shared values and interests are truly precious.
