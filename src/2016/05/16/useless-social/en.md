---
title: "Ineffective Socializing"
date: 2016-05-16 14:47:38 +0000
---

A year ago, Yahoo's Beijing R&D center shut down, and 36kr held a small event, so I went to join in the fun. That was when I started adding some people I had never met before. At the time, I met a founder whose WeChat friend list was already full, so we could not add each other. Over the past year, I have also run into people looking for ways to log into multiple WeChat accounts in order to increase the number of friends they can add. There are even dedicated solutions for this on Taobao, and Xiaomi's system has recently started supporting it too. This morning, I read an article on a public account called "Hello, Mr. Tree Hole," and it mentioned some similar views: in fact, even if you add many people, you do not truly have the energy to communicate with all of them.

At any given time, a person can only recognize and maintain a few hundred relationships. Modern tools make it easier for us to build connections, but those connections cannot all stay active. Here I want to ramble a bit and talk about what I think are different layers of interpersonal relationships.

1. Strangers. Usually people do not establish this kind of connection, but it does happen—for example, some businesses ask you to add the owner's WeChat, or you help a passerby take a photo and send them the picture. This kind of relationship is somewhere between optional and unnecessary, and it requires no special conditions. In many cases, though, both sides may choose not to look at each other's Moments or send messages, out of self-protection.
2. People you met for some reason, had a brief encounter with, did something together with, or can talk to because you are in the same industry. This kind of relationship is probably quite common. But in many cases, once the overlap ends, conversations become rare.
3. If both people are active users of Moments (frequently posting, or at least liking to browse), and they share some common understanding of articles, travel, or life, then the relationship can develop into one built on likes and occasional interaction. Browsing social feeds as a pastime does not consume too much energy from either side.
4. If one side does not post on Moments or participate at all, then that social relationship is basically similar to the stranger mode. Usually people who do not like participating in social feeds also rarely trigger deeper conversation. Since neither side knows what the other is up to, there is no basis for further interaction. Perhaps one day the other person remembers what you do, and if something comes up that can start a conversation, they may say a word to you.
5. If someone is overly enthusiastic, or sends content the other person is not interested in, or even fills the feed with all kinds of boring or disgusting things—like the annoying Moments posts from WeChat sellers—then most people will choose, at best, to mute them, or at worst, to delete them.
6. Ordinary classmates and friends. The difference from item 2 is that unless absolutely necessary, you would not delete each other as friends, though in practice the distinction may not be that big.
7. Best-friend level. You can send any message anytime, anywhere, and both sides are happy to communicate. When you send a message, you do not expect the other person to reply immediately, but they will still keep it in mind.

For me, to put it simply, I only start conversations when there is a reason to do so. Outside of best-friend-level relationships, that is the biggest summary. But if, in your chat history, you realize that you have been talking too much yourself, or the other person has not even replied to your previous message, deciding whether you should continue becomes a very painful process. After all, you do not know whether they were too busy to reply, forgot, or already dislike you. And then the meaning of that social relationship becomes much lower.

One point worth highlighting is that some people really are not sensitive to messages. They may easily overlook what you send and then never remember to reply. Well, people like that really are hard to get along with. For me, talking with them also feels tiring. Of course, that is just part of a person's personality—there is no real good, bad, right, or wrong in it.

Relationships are managed through effort from both sides. Their direction cannot be decided by one person's inclination alone. And even if one side really longs for a close relationship, they can only make a measured effort, and only as long as the other side does not dislike it.

Still, that sense of proportion is really hard to describe. Abstractly speaking, you could call it tact. For someone as straightforward as me, the engineering version would be something like implementing a retry mechanism in a program, doubling the delay after each retry...

Yeah—mutual understanding, letting things happen naturally. Relationships are not something you can force. Rather than building so many cold, useless social connections, it is better to put real care into the relationships you have with good friends.

Finding people with shared interests and temperament is truly precious.
