This National Day holiday, I finally managed to go on a trip for the first time this year. It was supposed to be a trip to mark the tenth anniversary of my friendship with Xiaobaiyun, and I more or less blindly signed up for a Beiwai 30 Degrees tour with him. This outdoor trip let me meet many new friends, which made me very happy.

What follows is a simple day-by-day account.

## Day 0

Beijing to Urumqi by plane, then the Grand Bazaar in Urumqi.

Before going to Xinjiang, I was still quite cautious. Because of the riots, everyone was worried about safety there. After arriving, it actually felt okay, though there were indeed many more security measures. Any place with heavy foot traffic had security checks. Besides airports and train stations, malls, markets, and hotels all had them too. Across from where we stayed was a wholesale dry-fruit market, and every vehicle entering had to open the trunk for inspection. Still, it was obvious that many of these checks were more for show than substance.

Urumqi's urban development is actually quite good. In my mind, Xinjiang used to mean yellow sand everywhere, but Urumqi has plenty of greenery. There are many overpasses in the city, and the style of the buildings is not much different from inland cities. Han Chinese still make up the majority of the crowd. The only things that really made me feel I was somewhere new were, first, that almost every road sign also had Uyghur text on it, and second, that the sun was two hours behind in the sky (yes, after a three-hour flight, the sun's height had barely changed).

There was still more than half a day left after arriving in Xinjiang, so I followed the group to the Grand Bazaar. "Bazaar" simply means market. There were all kinds of things sold there: naan, furs, fruit, handicrafts, and so on. Walking through the streets of Urumqi, it felt just like an inland city in some ways—lots of cars, traffic jams, and plenty of nice vehicles on the road. In the market, people did not seem especially afraid in terms of security, but there was not exactly a cheerful or joyful atmosphere either. In Xinjiang, things are sold by the kilogram rather than the half-kilo unit common inland. Fruit was indeed much cheaper, though since I'm not much of a foodie, I didn't buy anything—hehe—just ate some super-sweet Hami melon.

## Day 1

Today was mainly a driving day, from Urumqi to Habahe County.

The full journey was 680 kilometers and took more than 10 hours. There were security checks at gas stations all along the way, and in some places they even checked people, scanned ID cards, and took photos, though none of it was very strict.

![Road trip](/images/2016-10-07-img_5009.jpg)

The most unexpected thing was that for several hundred kilometers near Karamay there was 4G signal, and the network was very stable—something I hadn't even enjoyed in Xinjiang's capital.

We passed cotton fields and pretended to pick cotton. Xinjiang cotton plants are really short.

![Cotton field](/images/2016-10-07-img_5011.jpg) Cotton field

There were very few cars on the highway, giving it that classic road-movie feeling.

![Highway](/images/2016-10-07-img_5010.jpg) Highway

Passing grassland ranches, we saw quite a lot of cattle and sheep. If the grass had been green, it would have been even more beautiful.

![Grassland ranch, with lots of cattle and sheep](/images/2016-10-07-img_5012.jpg)

There was more water than I had imagined. In the end we entered the Irtysh River basin—the only river in China that flows into the Arctic Ocean.

![Road](/images/2016-10-07-img_5013.jpg) Road

## Day 8

Back to Beijing, and everyone went their separate ways. A faint sense of sadness lingered.

On the flight back, the GPS speed meter was pushed to its limit. A little geography explains it:

From the subtropical high-pressure belt to the polar low-pressure belt, the trade winds moving from the equator toward the poles are deflected by the Coriolis force, forming the westerlies around 30° to 60° north and south latitude. In the stratosphere they can reach 100 km/h. This is something from high school geography, and it is absolutely true. The economic cruising speed of a typical civil airliner is around 800 to 900 km/h.
