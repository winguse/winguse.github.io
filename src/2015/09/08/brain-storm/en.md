Today I was writing JS—more precisely JSX—but I was not really in the zone. After closing all tabs in Atom, I stared blankly at the editor, thoughts jumping around. On my way home from work, I ended up thinking about many things.

It has been roughly eight years since I wrote my first line of JavaScript. Back then, one of my projects was called "Guangzhou Daily Downloader." Its extension was not `.js` but `.jse`—many people probably do not even know that extension exists. My high school classmates may still remember it: in second year, I used it to download the Guangzhou Daily PDF every morning and merge pages into one big PDF for everyone to read between classes. Most people seemed to read sports more than news. The reason for `.jse` was that `.js` was already associated with web editors by default. Windows actually has many executable script/file types, like `vbs`, `vbe`, `js`, `jse`, `pif`, `com`, and `exe`. I also made a better-known `vbe` script called "PPT to Images," and another toy script called "VBSctipt Process Manager."

I looked at these files—last modified in 2007 or 2008—and tested them. Surprisingly, Microsoft's compatibility is still good enough that they run on Windows 10. Briefly, here is how they worked: "Guangzhou Daily Downloader" used the current date to enumerate PDF download URLs via XMLHTTP, then saved downloaded bytes with ADO.Stream. "PPT to Images" used FSO to enumerate PPT files in a directory and called Office DOM to save them as images. At that time, color-screen MP3/MP4 players were popular and classrooms were becoming digital, so people used my tool to import slides onto their devices. "VBSctipt Process Manager" used WMI APIs to get process lists, then terminated processes via WMI, `taskkill`, and NTSD. The motivation was simple: malware at that time often killed Task Manager as soon as it launched, and old Task Manager did not clearly show executable command lines and paths. I made it to simplify PC repair.

After all that, those old scripts are not even what I mainly want to talk about. What I want to highlight is their common trait: they were all scripts, and all used IE for the UI.

I mention this because Atom is built in a similar way. Its UI is essentially a browser. The difference is that it uses WebKit instead of IE, and V8 instead of Windows Script. More precisely, the project behind it is [Electron](http://electron.atom.io/), described as "Build cross platform desktop apps with web technologies." It feels ironic: I used the IE approach years ago out of necessity. I could not write VB or MFC, so after seeing an IE control example in some magazine, I hacked together UIs with the little table layout knowledge I had and built small tools.

Another thought is about Microsoft: a technical path that existed years ago only became widely realized later by another ecosystem, and is now used in many products. Which products? Microsoft's own VS Code, the popular productivity tool Slack, and others are all derived from [Electron](http://electron.atom.io/). Eight years ago, IE dominated. But Microsoft never strongly pushed IE forward, whether in W3C standards support or performance. Then Chrome appeared, versions advanced rapidly, and the web world changed dramatically over the past few years. [Electron](http://electron.atom.io/) gave web applications a new way to take over desktop application territory. After using Atom and VS Code, I keep asking myself: besides small size, what real advantage does Notepad++ still have?

From another angle, old IE had little motivation to improve performance—it was already the fastest and best in its own era. Back then, Mac and Linux users together were less than 1%, so cross-platform support was not urgent. But today the world has changed: faster browsers, faster scripting engines, more OS choices, and even the PC market giving major share to mobile. Microsoft also released Edge, whose performance is quite good. But I was a bit disappointed after hearing an internal talk saying they had not planned support for older Windows versions, and (for now) not for other platforms.

Microsoft has strengths: it built a mature ecosystem, and does stable work—a script from years ago can still run today with little issue. But other ecosystems are rising, and some of Microsoft's cross-ecosystem moves have felt less direct and decisive (though to be fair, it has become much more open in recent years). My feelings toward Microsoft are complex. As a new graduate joining Microsoft, I disliked the Windows terminal—at one point it did not even support resizing windows properly, and automation tooling lagged far behind \*nix. Yet C# and .NET were genuinely appealing. Looking at the mobile-internet era to now, my conclusion is: Microsoft did not fully win developers, and without developers, it could not fully win users.

I discovered [Electron](http://electron.atom.io/) a few days ago and looked through several interesting downstream products. But I have been so busy that I had little time to think deeply. That itself is a bit alarming: if we are too busy to think about things worth thinking about, how are we different from a salted fish drifting along?

Alright, enough rambling. If you are interested, here are [those old scripts](https://winguse.com/blog/2015/09/08/brain-storm/the_old_scripts/). I wrote them when I was young; I have not recovered properly indented, annotated versions, and they may not run perfectly anymore.
