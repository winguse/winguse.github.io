I was bored on the plane, so I am recording some simple deductions from economic models here. I call them deductions because they are only my personal one-sided reasoning and may not be correct. I simply want to analyze a problem through a partial and simple line of reasoning, and arrive at results that are at least somewhat close to the truth.

## Inflation and seigniorage

When a central bank issues currency, it generally does so through asset-backed issuance — that is, the central bank uses currency to buy certain assets, such as gold, and then gives currency to the asset holder.

For example, suppose someone has x grams of gold, and the central bank gives that person 100 units of currency in exchange. That completes the issuance of 100 units of currency. There are two ways to understand this. The simplest is as a commodity transaction, in which case there is no interest. The other is to understand it as the person using x grams of gold as collateral to obtain a loan of 100 units of currency. If we take the latter interpretation and assume an annual interest rate of 1%, then one year later, in order to “buy back” (redeem) the gold, this person would need 101 units of currency. If we assume the value of the gold does not change, then as a “general equivalent,” this can be understood as 1% inflation. This is seigniorage.

From another angle, because the gold is ultimately redeemed, the currency is actually also a kind of bond issued by the central bank, except that this bond has a negative yield.

## The central bank itself has gold..

The central bank can monetize its own gold, then lend the money directly to others and collect interest. The reason for inflation is the same as above.

## The central bank accepts deposits..

The central bank accepts deposits and pays deposit interest. It can then use those deposits to continue acquiring assets (making loans). The inflation logic is the same as above.

## After lending..

Suppose someone obtains a loan `y` from the central bank, purchases means of production, engages in production, then sells goods and receives `y * 101% + z` units of currency. The person repays the loan and deposits the money into the central bank (unless you hold money in your own hands, it exists in some form within the banking system). After `z` is deposited into the central bank, the central bank can continue acquiring assets, and the currency continues to circulate. `z` is the newly added money — that is, the value created by additional production.

You might ask: who has the extra `z` money to buy the value produced by that person? That additional money can first enter circulation in the form of loans as mentioned above, and then be deposited back into the central bank by the producer afterward. There is no need for a strict order here. (As long as assets and liabilities remain balanced, it is fine.)

## A bank run

Suppose confidence in the central bank collapses and everyone takes the currency they hold back to the central bank to redeem assets (for example, gold). Then the central bank can only return the assets acquired in the original issuance process and withdraw the currency. Because of inflation, 100 units of currency ultimately cannot redeem x grams of gold anymore; at least 101 units are needed to redeem the original amount of gold.

## What is the inflation portion used for?

Issuing currency has costs. Suppose the central bank issues 100 units of currency and earns 1 unit through inflation; that money can be used to pay the workers who print the money, buy paper, and so on.

## The quantity of money determines prices

The prices of goods fluctuate due to market factors, and one of those factors is how much money buyers have. On the macro level, that means how much money exists in the market. If there is more money, prices settled in money are higher; if there is less money, they are lower.

## Central bank market operations control the quantity of money

The central bank can control the amount of money in the market through market operations. By buying assets, it increases the money supply; by selling assets, it reduces the money supply. The central bank can also control the money supply by adjusting interest rates: when interest rates are high, idle funds are more likely to be deposited in banks, the central bank obtains deposits (money flows back), and the amount of money circulating in the market decreases.

Interest rates themselves are also affected by market factors: when bank deposit rates rise, other bond rates (such as government bonds) will also rise, because if you do not raise your rates, no one will buy your bonds.

## Commercial banks

The central bank cannot do everything itself, so various commercial banks take on some social functions, such as serving individuals and small businesses.

## Required reserves

Because issued currency eventually returns to banks, banks could in theory issue loans without limit. Claims are also a kind of asset, and if currency is issued against them, the amount of money would grow without bound. Loans also return to banks, so bank deposits would also grow without bound. Simply put, banks convert their own credit into society’s credit. If that credit collapses and there is a bank run, banks cannot immediately have enough assets to pay out — for example, in the production case above, it takes time to go from investing in production materials to producing goods to selling them. That creates systemic risk. Therefore, the monetary authority (the central bank) imposes a reserve requirement: whenever a commercial bank takes in a deposit, it must keep a certain proportion of that money at the central bank. This forms an infinite series, and in the end the amount of money in society converges to a fixed limit.

Taking all of the above together, the amount of money in society keeps growing. Part of the extra amount comes from inflation, but part of it also comes from the increase in value across society.

## Loans and economic growth

Loans can help producers expand production, earn more money, raise income, and improve living standards (that is, expand spending). One person’s spending is another person’s income, so the other person can also expand production or continue expanding spending. In this way, the economy grows.

But this game cannot continue forever. Investment always carries risk. Production may be expanded, but then natural disasters or man-made problems may occur, or products may become obsolete... That creates bad debt and reduces income. When these problems occur individually, they are not a big issue, because one person’s income can come from many others. But risks can accumulate, and once they reach a certain scale, they spread through the whole system, causing everyone’s income to shrink, overcapacity to become unsellable, and then an economic crisis arrives.

Economic crises are usually cyclical, because loans have maturities. When loans reach maturity and cannot be repaid, defaults occur, risks emerge, risks expand, and then a crisis appears. The usual solution to an economic crisis is merely to adjust the debt structure — for example, using new debt to pay off old debt. That is why you often see governments using various methods to ensure that some large debts do not default, so as to avoid triggering an even worse crisis. There is currently a form in China called “debt-to-equity swap,” which is also a way of transferring claims.

Of course, rolling over old debt with new debt cannot continue forever either. If those bad claims can be absorbed, then there is no problem: either economic growth covers that part (for example, the original debtor starts making money again), or the state issues government bonds to backstop it (for enterprises that are too big to fail), and in the end all taxpayers bear the cost... If even that cannot continue, then historically, large-scale war is what comes next.

## The United States collecting seigniorage from the whole world

As long as the U.S. dollar remains the main settlement currency for major goods in the world, the dollar will remain strong, and the risk of U.S. domestic debt can be digested by the whole world through the mechanisms described above. The credit of the dollar is backed by the economic, technological, and military strength of the United States (if you want to settle in some other currency, they will come after you). Or, more simply, the Federal Reserve can be regarded as the de facto central bank of the world.

## Tariffs and domestic inflation

Imposing high tariffs on American agricultural products will raise domestic prices of basic materials. For example, if soybean prices go up, soybean oil goes up, feed goes up, and then meat prices go up... A rise in one product can drive up the prices of similar products. In the end, costs across society rise, and people feel that money is worth less.

## Labor costs and exchange rates

In essence, labor creates value and the market determines price. Overcapacity creates excess supply and drives prices down; the opposite is also true. In terms of fairness, people anywhere in the world who make the same product should receive the same return, though reality is of course not like that. China’s current situation is that many industries have overcapacity, such as coal. When one industry has overcapacity, it pushes more industries into overcapacity — for example, cheap coal can make steel overcapacity as well. When the domestic market cannot absorb this capacity, one solution is to go overseas. To maintain export competitiveness, China has done many things such as export tax rebates and keeping the renminbi exchange rate relatively low. This is why you can see that domestic labor costs are very low (relatively speaking). Low prices are not necessarily the worst thing, because a low price is still better than no price. Even if you sell cheaply, at least you can still receive money and exchange it for other necessities of life. So in reality, Chinese working people have a relatively low standard of living and low income. Low income also leads to low consumption, so products in the market are not expensive and the cost of living is low.

The inflow of foreign exchange also makes it easier for the central bank to issue currency (a large part of China’s current money issuance comes from foreign exchange holdings; see the monetary authority balance sheet in the central bank’s annual reports). If China wants the renminbi exchange rate to remain competitive, it needs to reduce excess capacity. Low-end capacity lacks competitiveness, so what is needed is high-tech capacity.

## Taxation and government efficiency

On the macro level, an overall tax rate of x% for a country can be understood as x% of the population performing public services, and can also be understood as a reflection of the overall efficiency of that country (its government).
