## Basic background

There is a relatively detailed description of the whole event [on Wikipedia](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/反對逃犯條例修訂草案運動), so here I will roughly summarize it.

The cause was that in 2018, a young couple from Hong Kong traveled to Taiwan; the boyfriend killed the girlfriend and fled back to Hong Kong. Because there was no extradition procedure between Hong Kong and Taiwan, there was no way to bring the criminal to justice. The criminal's family asked the pro-establishment party [DAB](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%B0%91%E4%B8%BB%E5%BB%BA%E6%B8%AF%E5%8D%94%E9%80%B2%E8%81%AF%E7%9B%9F) for help, and the party submitted the amendment bill in February 2019. The main points of controversy in the bill were:

1. The bill was not only for the Taiwan case; it would also allow the Hong Kong government to provide extradition to mainland China, Macau, and Taiwan.
2. In the eyes of many Hong Kong people, the judicial system in mainland China is unjust. One of the most important concerns is that the mainland can fabricate charges arbitrarily, making people "become criminals."
3. Because Hong Kong people believe fabrication exists, it would be easy for mainland judicial power to extend into Hong Kong and undermine "one country, two systems."
4. For the Taiwan case specifically, there was already a process for one-off surrender of fugitives.

The bill went through the various procedures of Hong Kong's Legislative Council. Because the controversy was huge, the government made some concessions, such as reducing the number of applicable offenses and limiting it to serious crimes. But regarding the inclusion of mainland China within the scope of application, the government did not back down. In the Legislative Council, the pro-establishment camp [holds an overwhelming majority of seats (43/70)](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%AC%AC%E5%85%AD%E5%B1%86%E9%A6%99%E6%B8%AF%E7%AB%8B%E6%B3%95%E6%9C%83#%E6%94%BF%E9%BB%A8%E7%B5%84%E6%88%90), so opposition views from civil society could not be expressed through the legislature, and thus marches and protests emerged.

After multiple incidents, the public distilled five main demands:

1. Withdraw the Extradition Bill amendment
2. Retract the characterization of the June 12 and subsequent clashes as "riots"
3. Drop prosecutions and charges and release arrested protesters
4. Establish an independent commission of inquiry to investigate police abuse of force
5. Carrie Lam steps down, political reform is restarted, and universal suffrage for both the Chief Executive and the Legislative Council is implemented

The marches and protests continued, with large ones drawing from hundreds of thousands to two million people (numbers vary depending on organizers, police, and third-party statistics). From media reports across Hong Kong, the main body of the protests was peaceful, rational, and non-violent; but there were also serious clashes between police and civilians, with violence used by both sides, among which police overuse of force was reported and questioned by most media outlets.

After the protests kept erupting, Carrie Lam's government actually had many opportunities to cool things down regarding the trigger issue, but Carrie Lam used phrases like "suspend" and "die a natural death" while consistently avoiding the direct word "withdraw."

## Reporting by mainland media

Mainland media did not report on the movement in its early stages. After the event escalated, there was still little coverage. Only when it became serious did they begin one-sidedly stirring up patriotic sentiment. Mainland media hardly sent reporters to do frontline reporting.

The aspects that mainland media reported on or allowed discussion of were roughly as follows:

- The demands of the marches and protests were to pursue Hong Kong independence
- All participants in the marches and protests were rioters who used violence
- Hong Kong police were faithfully doing their duty and confronting rioters
- Hong Kong's main problem was mainly economic, with soaring housing prices
- Support the Hong Kong government
- The PLA/armed police gathering in Shenzhen
- The white-shirted men in Yuen Long were patriots teaching protesters a lesson

The aspects that mainland media did not report on or did not allow discussion of were roughly as follows:

- The five major demands and public opinion behind the marches
- When the marches began, how long they lasted, how many people participated, and that they were generally peaceful and rational
- Hong Kong police abuse of force
- The indiscriminate attacks by the white-shirted men in Yuen Long on everyone

## My views

### Public opinion in Hong Kong

Hong Kong's political camps are mainly divided into two: the pro-establishment camp and the democratic camp. Elections for the Legislative Council consist of two parts: geographical constituencies and functional constituencies (members chosen by social sectors). Because of the existence of functional constituencies, the pro-establishment camp holds most seats in the Legislative Council. In terms of media reports, both camps organized marches and rallies regarding the Extradition Bill. But activities supporting the amendment were far smaller in both number and scale than those opposing it. It is therefore not excessive to infer that public opinion in Hong Kong leaned toward opposing the bill. However, neither the Hong Kong government nor the mainland authorities ever seriously faced this public opinion, and instead kept acting against it.

There are also voices saying that people only took to the streets against the bill because someone paid them. I think this view is quite childish: it is truly hard to imagine that paying people could mobilize activities involving tens of thousands. On the other hand, since the pro-establishment camp holds the majority of seats in the Legislative Council, if they really represented more people, then they should have had more resources to organize activities larger than the opposition's or to stop them. Yet that did not happen.

From Carrie Lam's persistent refusal to use the word "withdraw," I can see a kind of foolish stubbornness.

### Public opinion under mainland censorship

The mainland side reported Hong Kong's movement in a one-sided way, using the habitual tactic of "either black or white": if you're part of the protest, you're "pro-independence" and violent. This stirred up hostility among mainland people toward Hong Kong citizens and created confrontation. Most mainland Chinese do not understand the matter itself and have no channel to understand it. Years of public-opinion guidance on the mainland have also created a stable inertia: most mainland Chinese tend to align with the mainland government's political stance and lack the habit of independent thinking. Even when given a chance, they are unwilling to understand opposing views, and without understanding them, they conclude those views are wrong and that contrary reporting is false.

Public opinion in mainland China is under strict control. Apart from official reports, any voices that deviate are blocked "according to regulations," and discussion is not permitted.

People always tend to accept opinions similar to their own and are reluctant to accept different ones. Therefore, for many mainland people, the preconditions for peaceful and rational discussion are already difficult to establish. A sorrow of the times.

### Forced political alignment

Some Cathay Pacific employees supported the anti-bill movement, and China's civil aviation regulator demanded a list of crew members on flights to mainland China. Under external pressure, Cathay Pacific dismissed some employees. Nominally, if pilots behave radically, there may be a risk to flight safety—for example, becoming terrorists—but in evaluating that possibility and making such a decision, I think it looked more like forcing an airline to take a political side.

This way of forcing political alignment is also how the mainland imposes pressure on many multinational companies, and some of these actions are not even grounded in law (for example, selective harassment through things like fire inspections). In my view, regardless of whether the motives are supposedly good, this is not the conduct of a civilized, law-based society. Mainland media has always condemned the United States' long-arm jurisdiction, but in essence there is no difference between the two. Moreover, the mainland's approach is also a form of forced value export.

### Violence

As for the protesters, I oppose all kinds of violence during protests, such as:

- Shining laser pointers into police officers' eyes and provoking the police
- Throwing bricks and debris
- Throwing Molotov cocktails and setting fires
- Damaging public facilities

(Regarding violent acts, there are also voices saying some violence was provoked by infiltrators sent by the government, but there is no direct evidence.)

At the same time, I also oppose protesters deliberately affecting other citizens, such as:

- Surrounding the airport and preventing travelers from passing through
- Blocking subway doors and preventing others from using the subway

As for the police, I condemn their frequent use of excessive force, such as:

- Arresting protesters and then still punching and kicking them after they had already fallen to the ground
- Using tear gas and rubber bullets without warning
- Using rubber bullets, pepper balls, and sponge rounds at close range
- Firing guns and throwing tear gas inside subway stations
- Continuing to use violence even after protesters had retreated
- Disguising themselves in plain clothes to provoke protesters and trigger incidents

If protesters violate relevant laws, they should be held accountable in court and accept punishment. But when police carry out operations, as a government department paid by taxpayers, they should rightly be subject to many restrictions, and their misconduct should be reasonably held accountable.

Hong Kong protesters can be divided into two groups: the "peaceful, rational, and non-violent" camp and the "valiant" camp, with the majority belonging to the former. Given such a huge base of participants, it is not incomprehensible that there would be some more radical members. But the two camps have not "split," because they believe their goals are the same and only their methods differ, and the more militant camp also knows it bears legal risk. I can understand this view as well.

When looking at an event, quite a few people neither support nor oppose it. The use of violence embarrasses supporters and pushes moderates away. I hope people in Hong Kong can recognize this rationally.

### Independence

I do not support Hong Kong independence. Hong Kong also does not possess the internal or external conditions for independence. First, based on the information currently available, the movement over these past few months was not for Hong Kong independence. Second, although there are voices calling for Hong Kong independence, most Hong Kong people do not want independence. Linking the movement of these past few months with Hong Kong independence is merely mainland media fanning the flames and manufacturing conflict. I strongly condemn this irresponsible behavior.

But I want to mention another point here: from 1997 to 2008, Hong Kong citizens' identification with the mainland was rising. Even before 1997, calls for Hong Kong independence were nowhere near as numerous as they are today. This is a question worth thinking about: why did Hong Kong society undergo such a change? I know the mainland side always fans the flames by talking about "foreign forces" and so on. I think even if such factors exist, they are definitely not the main factor. I have no conclusion, but I am considering some factors such as the scandal over earthquake donations since 2008, the shrinking of the mainland's speech environment, and Hong Kong people's efforts to realize the universal suffrage described in the Basic Law (the annual July 1 marches)...

### Feasibility of civil demands

At present, the entire anti-amendment movement still has no obvious leader, and the demands now being put forward do not really have such strong feasibility. I think the greatest art in political demands is compromise. I strongly support Hong Kong people expressing their demands, especially things like universal suffrage, which are written into the Basic Law. It is not that the "five major demands" are bad; it is just that if there is no willingness to compromise at all, that is not very realistic.

The core issue is "universal suffrage." In fact, if you scrutinize every word, the Basic Law leaves too much room for interpretation, giving the Hong Kong government and the mainland government plenty of room to pass the buck:

> Article 45 The Chief Executive of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region shall be selected by election or through consultations held locally and be appointed by the Central People's Government.
>
> The method for selecting the Chief Executive shall be specified in the light of the actual situation in the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region and in accordance with the principle of gradual and orderly progress. The ultimate aim is the selection of the Chief Executive by universal suffrage upon nomination by a broadly representative nominating committee in accordance with democratic procedures.
>
> The specific method for selecting the Chief Executive is prescribed in Annex I: "Method for the Selection of the Chief Executive of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region."

But according to

>
> (Adopted at the Tenth Session of the Standing Committee of the Twelfth National People's Congress on August 31, 2014)
>
> ...
>
> Starting from 2017, the election of the Chief Executive of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region may be implemented by the method of universal suffrage.
>
> ...

What happens after this government steps down remains to be seen.

### Hong Kong's substitutability for the mainland

In the short term, I do not see complete substitutability. With the opening and increase of mainland port cities and the establishment of multiple free trade zones, some of the functions originally provided by Hong Kong have been taken on elsewhere. But I still do not see short-term substitutability in the following two points:

- Hong Kong has independent customs status.

  Hong Kong is currently mainland China's third-largest export destination, after the United States and the European Union. Hong Kong itself is not capable of absorbing so many goods from the mainland. The main reason for such a large trading volume is that goods are exported to the rest of the world via Hong Kong. Broadly speaking, mainland goods can go through Hong Kong, get a label attached, and then be exported in Hong Kong's name, thereby avoiding many tariff barriers.

- Hong Kong allows free exchange of foreign currencies.

  The mainland implements foreign-exchange controls. Hong Kong's status as a financial center is something no mainland city possesses.

### The economic factors behind this movement

This is probably the only factor the mainland can talk about: roughly speaking, Hong Kong's economy has not been good in recent years and housing prices are extremely high. Unfortunately, this is not the main factor driving Hong Kong people into the streets. Regarding economic issues, I specifically looked up [World Bank data](https://www.google.com/publicdata/explore?ds=d5bncppjof8f9_&met_y=sp_pop_totl&idim=country%3ACHN%3AIND&hl=en&dl=en#!ctype=l&strail=false&bcs=d&nselm=h&met_y=ny_gdp_mktp_kd_zg&scale_y=lin&ind_y=false&rdim=region&idim=country:HKG:USA:CHN&ifdim=region&hl=en_US&dl=en&ind=false). You can see that, as a developed economy, although Hong Kong's GDP growth rate is not as high as the mainland's, it is still higher than that of the United States. No matter how dissatisfied Hong Kong people may be, it is not to the point that they would take to the streets solely for this reason.

But Hong Kong's economic problems are indeed serious. Its Gini coefficient has kept rising in recent years, and Hong Kong tycoons have captured most of the gains from economic growth. Some say Mr. Tung Chee-hwa was the only figure in Hong Kong's business world without a real-estate interest background, which is why he was able to push the "85,000 housing plan" at all, though unfortunately the resistance it faced was too great and it failed. Looking back at the "functional constituency" part of Legislative Council elections, it is not hard to see that these groups were set up for influential people in business circles. Naturally they represent the interests of the wealthy, and they are also the pro-mainland pro-establishment members.

So I think the core issue is still a political one: the current government does not have broad representativeness and does not represent the interests of ordinary people. This can also be seen from the scale of marches and rallies organized by the pro-establishment camp.

### Impact on Taiwan

This does not really need much elaboration; anyone who understands can see that a Hong Kong where the Basic Law is not implemented will push Taiwan further away from considering "one country, two systems." In recent years, many voices on the island of Taiwan (especially the current authorities) do not even recognize the "1992 Consensus" anymore. Regarding this refusal, Taiwanese people can even connect it to the mainland's recent attitude toward the Sino-British Joint Declaration.

As for this murder case, the current position of the Taiwanese authorities is to oppose the amendment of the Extradition Bill (mainly because they do not accept "One China" as the premise), so in fact the amendment has already deviated from its original purpose.

"People are watching how you act."

### The future of the mainland economy (hidden concerns)

In recent years, China's economic growth and the improvement in people's living standards are facts. I think a large part of this is due to the demographic dividend: the continuous increase in the working-age population has driven development in all aspects of society. But this kind of growth driven by piling up population cannot last in many respects. In ten or twenty years, without fundamental change, it is very likely to fall into the trap of population aging. Population also means market size. Right now China can use its market to export values, but after the demographic dividend fades, this trick may no longer work so well. This topic is too broad, and I do not have the ability to fully elaborate on it.
