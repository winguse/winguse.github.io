Come to think of it, 2020 was my tenth year of blogging. Unfortunately, I have not written much that feels truly useful. It has also been a long time since my last update, so here are some scattered thoughts.

After waking up, I kept following the newly reported pneumonia cases in Beijing. This time it feels very close to home, so the sense of crisis is stronger. Yesterday 1,900 people were tested, and this morning more than 500 sample results were released, with 45 positives—nearly 10%, which is frightening. China had gone for quite a while without local cases, yet things slipped in the capital. Of course, life still goes on and I still needed to go out for a meal. If you ask whether people seem worried, the streets look mostly unchanged, except that some security guards have started weakly checking passes and temperatures again. Beijing is huge, and so is Chaoyang District; and compared with overseas outbreaks, Beijing's single-digit daily numbers still seem orders of magnitude smaller.

Speaking of the pandemic, honestly it has had a significant short- and medium-term impact on me. For quite a while, I have felt deep uncertainty about the future and some anxiety about where to go next. I do not like self-comforting denial; the uncertainty I am talking about is mostly about work location and life changes, and compared with what many others are going through, it is not that much. Still, I am also anxious about the broader state of the world: the pandemic spreading globally, many people struggling, worsening geopolitical conditions, the U.S. and China shifting from cooperation to confrontation, rising populism, and even people blindly embracing so-called accelerationism. The world feels broken, and having no way to fix it only deepens that sense of helplessness.

This afternoon I watched a Bilibili creator discussing U.S. military bases in Japan and South Korea. I then dug deeper and realized the U.S. military is deployed almost everywhere in the world. I also browsed pages about U.S. overseas territories and found this article interesting: [51st state](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%BE%8E%E5%9C%8B%E7%AC%AC51%E5%B7%9E). I did not realize so many places had, at some point, wanted to become the 51st U.S. state. Of course, the term is also sometimes used sarcastically, to mock heavy Americanization. Among those cases, [Puerto Rico](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%B3%A2%E5%A4%9A%E9%BB%8E%E5%90%84) caught my attention: it is currently a U.S. unincorporated territory with self-government. It could pursue full independence, but several referendums have shown rising support for statehood. Meanwhile, the U.S. Congress has been reluctant, because adding one more state would dilute other states' voting weight. It gives me a sense of what true national influence looks like. Maybe the Tang dynasty once felt somewhat like this? I do not want to expand into a China–U.S. comparison here—just sharing what I learned today.

Since graduation, I have worked at wholly owned China subsidiaries of U.S. companies. Over the past two years of U.S.–China tension, teammates have often asked headquarters leaders what the impact might be on our Beijing team. Since our company currently has no domestic business in China and is only an R&D team—and because the company is small—the line "We are too small to matter" usually ends the discussion. I often suggest using softer wording: saying "Beijing" and "Washington" is better than saying "China" and "the U.S." Our SFO colleagues are generally very polite and friendly to PEK colleagues. Sometimes I feel we are just ordinary people living under the shadow of two major powers, yet we still exert subtle influence and bear some responsibility. As the saying goes, discrimination comes from prejudice, and prejudice comes from ignorance. People like us should help both sides understand each other and communicate, so confrontation can be reduced. Sadly, many people miss this point. In recent years especially, populism has surged, and people quickly escalate rhetoric—wolf-warrior diplomacy, great-power triumphalism, or impulsive mockery that fuels misunderstanding and hostility. In times like this, the more emotional you feel, the more calm you need to be.

Anyway, peace and development should still be the main theme of this world. I hope the world around us recovers soon.
